Lesson 01

Where this lecture fits: ACID, notation and today’s question

Week 07 asked what happens when a transaction fails. This lecture asks a different question: when transactions are interleaved, are the results still right?

From the lecture · slide 2

  • Consistency: today’s lecture.
  • Isolation is enforced by the concurrency control subsystem.
  • Durability is enforced by the recovery subsystem.

Today’s question

From the lecture · slide 6

No dumb questions
Why does consistency get a whole lecture and not a subsystem?
Each transaction, run alone, is written to keep the database consistent (Week 07). What can still break consistency is the order in which the operations of several transactions are mixed. This lecture gives the test that tells a safe order from an unsafe one.
What is the difference between checking and enforcing?
Checking: you are given a schedule and decide whether it is acceptable. Enforcing: the DBMS only lets acceptable schedules happen. This lecture is about checking; slide 6 leaves enforcing to the next lectures.

No dumb questions
Why is this not a dirty read?
A dirty read is reading a value that an uncommitted transaction has written. Here T2 reads X at position 2, before T1 writes it at position 3. T2 reads the original 80, which is a committed value. It is simply out of date by the time T2 writes.
Both transactions committed. How can the result be wrong?
Commit only says that a transaction finished and its changes are permanent. It does not say that the changes were computed from the right values. T2 computed 84 from a value of X that T1 was about to replace.
Lesson 03

Serial schedules: always safe, always slow

The simplest way to avoid every interleaving problem is not to interleave at all.

From the lecture · slide 7
  • A schedule S is serial iff the transactions’ operations are not interleaving.
  • Serial schedules:
    Sa: r1(X); w1(X); r1(Y); w1(Y); c1; r2(X); w2(X); c2;
    Sb: r2(X); w2(X); c2; r1(X); w1(X); r1(Y); w1(Y); c1;
  • Serial schedules don’t suffer lost updates, dirty reads…
  • Enforcing serial schedules reduces performance: T1 will have to wait for T2, or vice versa.

The numbers

The slide gives no values for the serial schedules. With the numbers of slide 5 (X = 80, Y = 20, N = 5, M = 4) they work out like this:

ScheduleXY
Start8020
Schedule A: T1 then T280 → 75 → 7920 → 25

The interleaved schedule of Lesson 0280 → 75 → 8420 → 25

Both serial schedules end with X = 79 and Y = 25. In a serial schedule each transaction sees the finished work of the one before it, so nothing can be overwritten half-way.

How to recognise a serial schedule

Read the schedule from left to right. In a serial schedule the operations of each transaction form one unbroken block: once the schedule has moved on to another transaction, the earlier one never appears again.

ScheduleSerial?Why
r1(X); w1(X); r1(Y); w1(Y); c1; r2(X); w2(X); c2;serialAll of T1, then all of T2
r2(X); w2(X); c2; r1(X); w1(X); r1(Y); w1(Y); c1;serialAll of T2, then all of T1
r1(X); r2(X); w1(X); r1(Y); w2(X); c2; w1(Y); c1;not serialT2 runs in the middle of T1
r1(X); w1(X); r2(X); w2(X); r1(Y); w1(Y);not serialT1 is cut in two by T2. (This is Schedule D of Lesson 07. It is not serial, and it is still correct.)

Any serial order is accepted as correct extra

With two transactions there are two serial schedules, and in this example they happen to give the same result. That is not always so. Take T1: X := X + 10 and T2: X := X * 2 with X = 100. T1 then T2 gives (100 + 10) × 2 = 220. T2 then T1 gives 100 × 2 + 10 = 210. Both are accepted: each one is what you get when the transactions run one at a time, and the DBMS never promised which one would go first.

With n transactions there are n! serial schedules: 2 for two transactions, 6 for three, 24 for four.

The price of serial schedules

Week 07 listed what serial-only execution costs: low throughput, long response time and poor use of the CPU and disks. If T1 is waiting for a disk read, T2 has to wait as well, even when T2 touches completely different data.

Watch it!Serial is something you can see by looking at the order of the operations: no interleaving. It is not the same word as serializable, which is the subject of the next lesson.
Brain powerWhich of these is serial? (a) r1(X); w1(X); c1; r2(Y); w2(Y); c2; r3(X); c3; (b) r1(X); r2(Y); w1(X); w2(Y);

Answer: (a) is serial: three blocks, T1, T2, T3. (b) is not serial, because T2 starts before T1 has finished. It is harmless all the same: T1 uses only X and T2 only Y.
No dumb questions
Do the two serial schedules have to give the same result?
No. Each serial schedule is correct on its own terms. Schedule A and Schedule B agree here only because subtracting 5 and adding 4 can be done in either order.
If serial schedules are always safe, why are they not simply enforced?
Because of performance. Slide 7 says it directly: T1 will have to wait for T2 or vice versa. The rest of the lecture looks for schedules that are interleaved and still safe.
Lesson 04

The goal and the idea: serializable schedules

We want the speed of interleaving and the safety of serial execution. Serializability is the bridge between the two.

From the lecture · slides 8 and 9
  • Goal: a non-serial schedule, to improve the performance, that still leaves the database consistent. In the slide’s words: to get a valid non-serial schedule.
  • Idea: check if the non-serial schedule is equivalent to a serial schedule.
  • A schedule that passes this check is a serializable schedule.

The definition

From the lecture · slide 10A schedule S of n transactions is serializable iff
  • it is equivalent to another schedule S′, and
  • S′ is a serial schedule of the same n transactions.

Read it slowly. S itself can be as interleaved as you like. What matters is that there is some serial schedule of the same transactions that S is equivalent to. A serial schedule is correct, so a schedule that is equivalent to one is correct too.

Attention · slide 11
  • Being serializable is not the same as being serial.
  • Being serializable implies that the schedule is a correct schedule.
  • It will leave the database in a consistent state.
  • The interleaving is appropriate and will result in a state as if the transactions were serially executed, yet will achieve efficiency due to concurrent execution.

Brain powerA schedule of three transactions is serializable. How many serial schedules are there to compare it with, and how many of them must it match?

No dumb questions
Is every serial schedule serializable?
Yes. A serial schedule is equivalent to itself, and it is a serial schedule of the same transactions. Serial schedules are the simplest serializable schedules.
Is every serializable schedule serial?
No, and that is the whole point. Schedule D in Lesson 07 is interleaved and serializable. Slide 11 says it in one line: being serializable is not the same as being serial.
Which serial schedule must S be equivalent to?
Any one of them. The definition only asks that such a serial schedule S′ exists.

1. Result equivalence

From the lecture · slide 13Two schedules are result-equivalent if they produce the same final database state.

It sounds like exactly what we want. The slides test it on two tiny schedules. Each one reads X, changes it and writes it back:

From the lecture · slides 13 and 14
  • With X = 100: S1 and S2 are result equivalent!
  • Not acceptable! S1 and S2 are result equivalent for some values, not for all values.

Why it is rejected

Adding 10 and multiplying by 1.1 are different calculations. They give the same answer only when X + 10 = 1.1 × X, which happens at X = 100 and nowhere else. The match on slide 13 is an accident of the value that happened to be in the database.

A test for schedules cannot depend on such accidents. The same schedule runs on many different database states, so “equivalent” has to mean equivalent whatever the values are. Running every possible database state is impossible. What we can inspect is the operations and their order, and that is how the next definition works.

Key pointThe same final state for one starting value proves nothing. An acceptable definition of equivalence looks at the operations, not at the numbers.
Brain powerFor which starting value do X := X + 5 and X := X * 2 give the same final state?

Answer: Only X = 5 (both give 10). X + 5 = 2 × X has one solution. For every other value the two are different, so calling them equivalent would be wrong.
No dumb questions
Is “the same final result” not exactly what we are after?
Yes, but for every possible database state, and without having to try them all. Conflict equivalence gives that guarantee by looking only at the order of the operations.
Are S1 and S2 two schedules of the same transaction?
No. One adds 10 and the other multiplies by 1.1. That is what makes the example a warning: two unrelated programs can look equivalent if you only compare one final state.
Lesson 06

Conflict equivalence

Forget the values. Look only at which operations can interfere with each other, and at the order in which they happen.

Reminder: conflicting operations

Rule from Week 07Two operations conflict iff (1) they belong to different transactions, (2) they access the same data item, and (3) at least one of them is a write. (Week 07 guide, Lesson 07.)
Earlier operationLater operationConflict?Why

The method

1Check that the two schedules contain the same operations.
↓
2In the first schedule, list every conflicting pair. Work one data item at a time so that none is missed.
↓
3Find the same two operations in the second schedule. Is the same one still first?
↓
4Every pair in the same order: conflict-equivalent. One pair reversed: not conflict-equivalent.

Watch it!
  • Two reads never conflict. Do not list r1(X), r2(X) as a pair.
  • Two operations of the same transaction never conflict.
  • Compare the order, not the positions. A pair may sit at positions 1 and 5 in one schedule and 1 and 6 in the other and still be in the same order.
  • One matching pair proves nothing. All pairs must keep their order.
Brain powerT1 reads and writes only X. T2 reads and writes only Y. Are all the schedules of these two transactions conflict-equivalent to each other?

The serialization (precedence) graph

One small drawing answers the whole question: is this schedule conflict-serializable?

From the lecture · slide 18Serialization graph (precedence graph): a tool that is used to tell whether a given schedule S is conflict-serializable. A graph consists of nodes and edges.

How to build it

From the lecture · slide 19
  1. Create a node for each transaction.
  2. Create an edge (T1 → T2) if there is w1(x) before r2(x).
  3. Create an edge (T1 → T2) if there is r1(x) before w2(x).
  4. Create an edge (T1 → T2) if there is w1(x) before w2(x).
Schedule S is conflict-serializable iff the serialization graph has no cycles.

Rules 2, 3 and 4 are the three kinds of conflicting pair from Lesson 06. The slide writes them for T1 and T2; they hold for any two transactions Ti and Tj. In one sentence: for every conflicting pair, draw an arrow from the transaction whose operation comes first to the transaction whose operation comes second.

Found in the scheduleKind of conflictEdge
wi(x) … then … rj(x)write-read (rule 2)Ti → Tj
ri(x) … then … wj(x)read-write (rule 3)Ti → Tj
wi(x) … then … wj(x)write-write (rule 4)Ti → Tj
ri(x) … then … rj(x)no conflictno edge

What an edge means

An edge Ti → Tj is a demand: in any serial schedule that is conflict-equivalent to S, Ti must come before Tj, because that is the only way to keep that conflicting pair in the same order.

A cycle T1 → T2 → T1 says “T1 before T2” and “T2 before T1” at once. No serial order can do both, so no conflict-equivalent serial schedule exists. Without a cycle, the transactions can always be lined up so that every arrow points forward, and that line-up is the equivalent serial schedule.

View equivalence, the scheduler and the map of schedule types

Three closing ideas: a looser kind of equivalence, who enforces all of this, and how the schedule types fit inside each other.

View equivalence: the idea only

From the lecture · slide 25
  • A less restrictive definition of equivalence of schedules.
  • The premise behind view equivalence: as long as each read operation of a transaction reads the result of the same write operation in both schedules, the write operations of each transaction must produce the same results.
  • “The view”: the read operations are said to see the same view in both schedules.

Conflict equivalence fixes the order of every conflicting pair. View equivalence asks for less: each read must get its value from the same write as before. Because it asks for less, more schedules pass it. Slide 12 marks view equivalence as “not in this course”, so the test itself is not covered; what you need is the idea and its place in the picture below.

From the lecture · slide 26Transaction processor (scheduler):
  • Forces the order of operations’ execution to guarantee a certain schedule type. Mostly, a serializable schedule.

Exam method, worked exam questions, key ideas & dictionary

Everything on one page: the method, two real exam questions solved with it, the mistakes to avoid and the vocabulary.

How to answer “Is S conflict-serializable?”

1Number the operations of S from left to right.
↓
2Draw one node per transaction.
↓
3For each data item, list its operations in order. Skip items used by one transaction and items that are only read.
↓
4For each pair from different transactions with at least one write, draw the arrow earlier → later and label it with the item.
↓
5Look for a cycle.
↓
6Write the conclusion. No cycle: “conflict-serializable, equivalent to the serial schedule …”. Cycle: “not conflict-serializable, because of the cycle …”.
What a full answer contains
  • The conflicting pairs that create each arrow (at least one per arrow).
  • The graph, with the items written on the arrows.
  • The verdict, in the lecture’s words: conflict-serializable or not conflict-serializable.
  • The reason: the equivalent serial order (all of them, if the question asks), or the cycle.
  • If the question also asks “is it serial?”: say whether the transactions’ operations are interleaved.

Common mistakes
  • Calling a schedule “not serializable” because it is not serial. Interleaved schedules can be serializable.
  • Drawing an arrow for two reads, or for two operations of the same transaction.
  • Drawing the arrow from the later operation to the earlier one.
  • Stopping at the first item. A cycle can need two items, as in Schedule E (X one way, Y the other).
  • Giving “conflict-serializable” without the equivalent serial order, or “not conflict-serializable” without naming the cycle.
  • Mixing up the two tests: the graph says nothing about recoverable, cascadeless or strict.

Key ideas

  • Recoverable is not enough. The slide 4 schedule is recoverable and cascadeless and still loses an update.
  • Serial: no interleaving. Always correct, but transactions wait for each other.
  • Serializable: equivalent to some serial schedule of the same transactions. Correct, and it keeps the benefit of concurrency.
  • Result equivalence is not acceptable: it may hold for some values and not for others.
  • Conflict equivalence: the order of every conflicting pair is the same in both schedules.
  • Conflict-serializable: conflict-equivalent to a serial schedule. Test it with the serialization graph.
  • Graph: a node per transaction; an edge for w…r, r…w and w…w on the same item, from the earlier transaction to the later one.
  • No cycle means conflict-serializable, and the graph gives the serial order. A cycle means not conflict-serializable.
  • Serial ⊂ Conflict-serializable ⊂ View-serializable.
  • The scheduler guarantees the schedule type with concurrency control protocols.

46A schedule is serial iff the transactions’ operations are not interleaved: each transaction runs completely before the next one starts, for example r1(X); w1(X); r1(Y); w1(Y); c1; r2(X); w2(X); c2; A schedule S of n transactions is serializable iff it is equivalent to another schedule S′ that is a serial schedule of the same n transactions. A serializable schedule may be interleaved. It is still a correct schedule: it leaves the database in a consistent state, as if the transactions had been executed serially, and it keeps the efficiency of concurrent execution. Serial schedules do not suffer lost updates or dirty reads, but enforcing them reduces performance, because T1 has to wait for T2 or vice versa.
47Two schedules are result-equivalent if they produce the same final database state. With X = 100, S1 gives 110 and S2 gives 110, so they look equivalent. With X = 90, S1 gives 100 and S2 gives 99. They are result-equivalent for some values and not for all values, so the definition is not acceptable: it depends on the values that happen to be in the database, not on what the schedules do. The lecture uses conflict equivalence instead.
48Two operations conflict if they belong to different transactions, access the same data item, and at least one of them is a write. Two schedules are conflict-equivalent if the order of any two conflicting operations is the same in both. A schedule is conflict-serializable if it is conflict-equivalent to a serial schedule. Schedule A, r1(X); w1(X); r1(Y); w1(Y); r2(X); w2(X); is serial. Schedule D, r1(X); w1(X); r2(X); w2(X); r1(Y); w1(Y); is interleaved. 49(1) Create a node for each transaction. (2) Create an edge T1 → T2 if w1(x) comes before r2(x). (3) Create an edge T1 → T2 if r1(x) comes before w2(x). An edge Ti → Tj means that Ti must come before Tj in any conflict-equivalent serial schedule. A cycle such as T1 → T2 → T1 would need T1 before T2 and T2 before T1, which no serial schedule can give. 50View equivalence is a less restrictive definition of equivalence. The scheduler (transaction processor) forces the order of the operations’ execution to guarantee a certain schedule type, mostly a serializable schedule, and it does this with concurrency control protocols. The types are nested: every serial schedule is conflict-serializable and every conflict-serializable schedule is view-serializable (Serial ⊂ Conflict-serializable ⊂ View-serializable). The reverse statements are false.
